\begin{abstract}
\noindent The main paper reduces 235 careers to a single number. This companion
puts the people back. It carries a profile row for every person in the study ---
highest completed degree, field, institution, nationality, sex and area of
expertise --- a chapter of descriptive statistics over those fields, and a
chapter of individual cases chosen because their numbers argue with the median.

Degree and institution facts were lifted from the sourced notes already attached
to each row in \texttt{data/anchors.csv} (86\% of rows) and researched fresh only
where those notes were silent. \emph{unknown} appears where nothing was found;
the study does not guess, and 16 people have no sourced credential at all.

\vspace{0.6em}
\noindent\textbf{The same caveat governs every number here.} These are people
who already succeeded. The sample has no denominator, so nothing in this
document is a rate or a forecast --- see the main paper's glossary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Some interesting cases}

These are not the most famous people in the sample. They are the ones whose
numbers argue with the median, and each is here because it teaches something the
pooled figure hides.

\subsection*{The ones who took a lifetime}

\textbf{John Hopfield} --- neural networks, PhD, hit at \textbf{91}. Sixty-six
years from finishing his doctorate to the 2024 Nobel. The associative memory
network he described in 1982 was recognised four decades later, when hardware
finally made the idea matter. His row is the single longest clock in the study
and a clean demonstration that recognition dates the world's readiness, not the
work.

\textbf{Tu Youyou} --- artemisinin, bachelor's degree, hit at \textbf{85}. She
isolated the compound in the early 1970s inside a classified military
programme, without a doctorate, a postdoc, or foreign training. The Nobel came in
2015. Her clock is 60 years and every one of them is an artifact of who was
allowed to be recognised and when.

\textbf{Rita Levi-Montalcini} --- nerve growth factor, MD, hit at 77. Began the
work in a bedroom laboratory in wartime Italy after racial laws barred her from
academic posts. The study cannot represent what that start cost, only that the
interval was 50 years.

\subsection*{The ones who took almost no time}

\textbf{Katrina Lake} --- apparel e-commerce, hit \textbf{1.5 years} after her
MBA, at 30. The shortest education-to-hit clock in the commercial data. She had
worked in retail analytics and venture capital first; the MBA sat late in a
career already pointed at the problem, which is exactly why the clock reads short.
A reminder that a short clock often means the anchor moved, not that the work
was quick.

\textbf{Austin Russell} --- lidar, no degree, hit at \textbf{20}. Left Stanford
on a Thiel Fellowship having started the company at 17. He and Aadit Palicha
(quick commerce, hit at 20) are the two youngest commercial crossings in the
study, and both skipped the credential entirely rather than finishing it fast.

\textbf{Frank Wang} --- consumer drones, hit 1.5 years out of his master's at 32.
The company was founded in his dormitory; the degree and the venture overlap
rather than sequence. Several of the fastest clocks in this study share that
shape, and it is worth noticing that ``fast'' usually means \emph{concurrent},
not \emph{efficient}.

\subsection*{The ones with no degree at all}

Eighteen people crossed \$10M-constant with a high-school credential or less.
The set is more varied than the stereotype:

\textbf{Zhou Qunfei} left school at 16 to work in a watch-lens factory in
Shenzhen and crossed at 34 in cover-glass manufacturing --- a capital-intensive
industrial business, not a software one. \textbf{Amancio Ortega} began as a shirt
shop errand boy and crossed at 32. \textbf{Robert Kuok} (sugar trading, 37) and
\textbf{Martua Sitorus} (palm oil, 32) both built commodity trading houses.
\textbf{Diane Hendricks} crossed at 37 in building-materials distribution after
starting in custom home building.

The pattern worth extracting: the no-degree cohort is concentrated in
\emph{physical} businesses --- manufacturing, distribution, trading, retail ---
where the barrier is operational competence and capital rather than credentialed
knowledge. The famous college-dropout software founders are the minority of this
group, not its archetype.

\subsection*{The career switchers}

\textbf{Sara Blakely} sold fax machines door to door for seven years before
founding a shapewear company with no apparel background whatsoever. Her row was
recruited into the study precisely to stress the assumption that the pre-venture
decade must be spent in-domain.

She is not an outlier. One in five of the winners entered their eventual field
three or more years after finishing education, with a median detour of 4.5
years, and their median age at hit --- 34.2 --- is under two years behind those
who went straight in. \textbf{The detour is survivable and common.} What the data
does not show is any evidence that it \emph{helps}.

\subsection*{The one whose date is a warning}

\textbf{Mark Zuckerberg}'s crossing is dated 2005, and the paper flags it as the
weakest-supported figure in that wave. The 2005 revenue figure comes from a
single trade-press sentence quoting an S-1 that does not itself disclose
anything before 2007; the margin above the bar is 48\%, the narrowest in its
batch. If that one sentence is wrong, the crossing moves to 2006.

It is included because the alternative --- dropping every row whose evidence is
thin --- would bias the sample toward the well-documented, which is the very
failure mode the study spent the most effort fighting. It is called out here so
that anyone quoting his row quotes the uncertainty with it.

\subsection*{The pair that changed the method}

\textbf{Donna Dubinsky} and \textbf{Jeff Hawkins} both date to 1995 at Palm
Computing. Two people, one crossing. Because the bootstrap treats every row as
an independent draw, correlated rows narrow the confidence interval without
adding information --- the estimate looks more precise than the evidence
supports.

Finding them led to a rule change mid-study: one row per hit \emph{event},
entity plus year, not per person. On its first use the rule rejected five
candidates, including a brother pair who founded one pharmaceutical company and
two co-holders of a single Nobel year. Both Palm rows were kept because they sit
outside the headline set and cannot affect the reported interval --- and because
dropping one would have required inventing a rule for choosing between a founder
and the CEO who built it, which correlates with sex in this sample.

\subsection*{The youngest crossings are not entrepreneurs}

\textbf{Billie Eilish} crossed at \textbf{15} and \textbf{Olivia Dean} at 20 ---
both on the audience criterion rather than revenue. Creative careers can reach a
million-person audience before the person is old enough to sign a contract
alone, and no business in this dataset does anything comparable.

If you are drawn to the very young hits in this study, note which criterion they
fired on. The youngest \emph{commercial} crossings are 20, and there are two of
them out of 121.
