\section*{Read this before any number below}

You collected this data. You already know the caveat, so this is the short
version: \textbf{the median is a duration, not a probability.} It says how long
the road ran for people who reached the end. It says nothing about how many set
out.

That has a specific consequence for the decision in front of you. This study
\textbf{cannot} tell you whether to do a PhD or start a company, because it
never sampled anyone who tried either and failed. Both arms of your decision are
represented here only by their winners. Any sentence of the form ``the data says
path A beats path B'' is unsupported, and I am not going to write one.

What the data \emph{can} do is describe the shape and timing of careers that
worked. That is genuinely useful for planning, and useless for forecasting.

\section{The four numbers that bear on your decision}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
Quantity & Median & Spread \\
\midrule
Age at first hit & \textbf{33.0} & IQR 30--39, $n$=105 \\
\quad if the hit landed post-1995 & 32.0 & IQR 28.8--36.0 \\
Years from age 18 to first hit & 15.0 & IQR 12--21 \\
\textbf{Years from starting the venture that worked} & \textbf{4.2} & IQR 3.0--7.2, $n$=118 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

You are 22. The median person in this dataset who eventually crossed \$10M
constant did it at 33. That is eleven years from where you stand, and
\textbf{both of your options fit inside it.} A five-year PhD ending at 27 leaves
you six years to the median. Starting a company now leaves you eleven.

Nothing about the timing forces the choice. That is the first useful finding: on
this evidence, the decision is not a race against a closing window.

\section{The reframe: the long pole is not the company}

Put two of those numbers next to each other. From age 18 to the hit is a median
of 15 years. From \emph{starting the venture that worked} to the hit is 4.2.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Roughly eleven of those fifteen years happen before the winning venture
starts.}
\end{quote}

The company is the short part. The long part is whatever you were doing
beforehand --- and in this sample, that period is not idle. It is where domain
knowledge, capital, credibility and the earlier failed attempts accumulate. The
study's own rule that a serial founder is dated at their \textbf{first} company
to cross, not their famous one, means those earlier attempts are inside the
eleven years, not excluded from them.

So the real question is not ``PhD or company.'' It is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Which pre-venture decade puts me in a position where a venture takes four
years instead of never working at all?}
\end{quote}

A PhD is one way to spend part of that decade. So is building and failing twice.
So is working for someone competent in an industry you intend to attack. This
data cannot rank them. But it does say the pre-venture period is where the time
actually goes, which means choosing it casually is the expensive error --- not
choosing it slowly.

\section{Where the two paths diverge in the data}

The one place this dataset speaks directly to research-versus-commerce is the
venture clock by field, and it is stark:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Field & $n$ & Median years from venture start to hit \\
\midrule
Software / internet & 54 & 4.0 \\
Hardware / deep tech & 33 & 4.0 \\
\textbf{Science / research} & 10 & \textbf{14.0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Read this carefully, because the obvious reading is wrong.} These are
not the same test. For the commercial fields, the hit is \$10M of revenue in
constant dollars --- a good business, achieved by thousands of people per
decade. For science, the hit criterion is a \textbf{major prize}: a Nobel, a
Fields Medal, a Turing Award, a Breakthrough Prize. That is a handful of people
per year on the planet.

So the honest statement is: \emph{reaching the very top of academic science
takes about fourteen years from starting the work, while building a
\$10M business takes about four from starting the company.} Those are not
comparable outcomes and the ratio between them is not a return on investment. If
anything the comparison flatters research, because the science rows are sampled
at a far more extreme threshold and still show up in a dataset this small.

What you can take from it: \textbf{academic recognition is a slow clock even for
its winners}, and the sample contains nobody who got there quickly. Also note
$n$=10. Treat that 14.0 as an impression, not a measurement.

Also relevant: software has the youngest median age at hit in the study, 30.0,
against 34.5 for hardware and 36.0 for healthcare. Fields differ in when they
pay, by five or six years.

\section{The factor this data cannot see, and it may dominate}

Your decision has a structural asymmetry the dataset has no column for:
\textbf{immigration status.}

Nobody in this sample was selected on visa constraints. Several were immigrants
--- but the study recorded where their career ran, never what permitted it to
run there. That is a real hole for you specifically, and it cuts one way:

\begin{itemize}
\item A US PhD is not only training. It is roughly five more years of lawful
presence, an assistantship that pays, OPT afterwards, repeated H-1B lottery
entries, and eligibility arguments for EB-1B or an EB-2 national interest waiver
that a bachelor's alone does not support.
\item Going home to Busan after the bachelor's to build is not a neutral pause.
It substantially closes the US route for years, and reopening it later is
harder than not closing it.
\item A US business started on F-1 has narrow lawful room to operate before OPT,
and that constraint is on the timeline whether or not the business is good.
\end{itemize}

I want to be precise about the logic here: \textbf{this is not an argument that
the PhD is better.} It is an argument that the two options are not symmetric in
what they foreclose. One of them keeps more branches open at roughly equal cost
in years. When a decision is genuinely close on the merits --- and on this
evidence it is --- optionality is the tiebreaker that survives being wrong.

The camera-import business is the interesting third case precisely because it is
the one that could plausibly run from either country, which makes it the option
least coupled to this asymmetry. That is worth more than it sounds.

\section{What would actually change my read}

Rather than a recommendation, here is what I would want you to answer, because
each one moves the decision more than any number in this study:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Do you have a specific thing to build, or a category?} Everyone in
this dataset crossed with a particular product for a particular buyer. ``Start a
business'' is not a plan and the four-year venture clock does not apply to it.
If the answer is a category, the eleven pre-venture years have not started
being spent well yet.
\item \textbf{Is the PhD in something adjacent to what you would build?} Physics
to deep tech or instrumentation keeps both paths alive at once. Physics to
academic physics is a commitment to the 14-year clock and its far narrower gate.
\item \textbf{Can you test the business inside the next twelve months without
giving anything up?} The camera-import idea is unusually testable --- it has
real customers, real margins, and a real cash cycle you can observe. A quarter
of genuine trading tells you more than this entire study can.
\item \textbf{What happens if you are still pre-hit at 33?} The median says
half of the winners were. Whichever path leaves you employable, solvent and in a
country you chose at 33 is the one that survives the median outcome rather than
the lucky one.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The honest summary}

The data supports exactly three statements about your decision:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{You are not late, and there is no closing window.} The median
winner crossed at 33; the middle half crossed between 30 and 39.
\item \textbf{The venture is the short part.} About eleven of the fifteen years
from age 18 happen before the winning company starts. Choose the pre-venture
decade deliberately; that is where the time actually goes.
\item \textbf{Top-of-field academic recognition is a slow clock even for
winners}, roughly fourteen years from starting the work, on thin evidence and at
a far more extreme threshold than the commercial rows.
\end{enumerate}

Everything past that is not in the data. The visa asymmetry is real and points
toward keeping US presence. The strongest argument for building now is having
something specific to build, and this study cannot tell you whether you do.

\emph{\small One last time, because it is the misreading this dataset invites
most: nine years, or thirty-three, is how long it took the people who made it.
It is not your odds, and it is not a schedule. The people who spent the same
decade and got nothing are not in this file --- not because they were rare, but
because the study was built in a way that cannot see them.}
