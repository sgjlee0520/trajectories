\title{\bfseries How Long Before the First Hit?\\[0.35em]
\large A conditioned-on-success estimate of time from education to a first
\$10M-constant crossing, with its biases enumerated}
\author{\normalsize Study repository: \texttt{\textasciitilde/trajectories}}
\date{\normalsize 23 August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent We collected dated career anchors for 235 people drawn only from
enumerated third-party lists, and measured the interval from the end of formal
education to the first year an individual's own venture crossed \$10 million in
\textbf{constant 2026 dollars}. Among the 93 people for whom that interval is
computable on primary revenue evidence, the median is \textbf{9.0 years}, 95\%
bootstrap CI \textbf{[8.0, 10.0]}. Collection stopped under a pre-specified rule
on median stability and interval width.

The estimate describes only people who eventually crossed. Those who worked for
decades and never crossed are absent by construction, and the two strongest
measured biases both push the figure short. \textbf{The number is a floor, not a
forecast.} The finding we consider most useful is not the pooled median but its
era split: careers whose hit landed before 1995 took a median of 13.0 years,
those after 1995 took 8.0, and the sample is only 26\% pre-1995.
\end{abstract}

\section{What was measured}

For each person we recorded six dated anchors, each with a source URL and a
confidence grade: birth; the end of formal education (\emph{last completed
credential}, so a dropout is dated to their previous completed credential, not
to the year they left); first paid work in the field the hit came from; first
independent venture; \textbf{first hit}; and a much later scale milestone.

The headline clock is education-end to first hit. Two alternative clocks --- from
age 18, and from first venture --- are reported in Section~\ref{sec:robust}
because they survive different missing data.

\subsection{The threshold is inflation-adjusted, and this matters more than it sounds}

A hit is \$10 million in annual revenue \textbf{in constant 2026 dollars}. A
nominal \$10M threshold is not the same test across eras: crossing \$10M in 1960
required a business roughly eleven times larger in real terms than crossing it
today. The 1960 bar is \textbf{\$919{,}417}. Applied to a sample that is 26\%
pre-1995 by construction, a nominal threshold would have inflated exactly the
careers this frame works hardest to include.

This was not a theoretical concern. In an early pilot a single row was dated
eleven years late because a nominal \$10M was applied to a 1960s company. Every
revenue year in the final data was checked against that year's computed bar, and
each row's notes carry the pasted output of the conversion tool for the year
used.

\subsection{Selection}

Names entered only by enumerating a named third-party list in its published
order --- rankings in rank order, prizes in year order --- and taking entries as
they came. Country, era, and sex were recorded but never used to select. The
sole standard for entry is the money criterion.

The pooled 9.0 is not a stable property of ambitious careers. It is a weighted
average of two eras that differ by five years, in a sample that is 74\%
post-1995. Anyone applying this number to a career today should read the 8.0,
and anyone applying it historically should read the 13.0. Reporting the pooled
figure alone would be the single most misleading thing this dataset permits.

We do not claim the mechanism. Faster capital formation, software distribution
economics, and better documentation of recent private revenue would all produce
this pattern, and the last of those is a measurement artifact rather than a
change in the world.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

\subsection{Missing education dates remove 23\% of the eligible sample}

Of 121 people with a hit dated on primary revenue evidence, \textbf{28 have no
sourced education-end year} and therefore cannot enter the headline clock. This
is the largest single threat to the estimate and it is structural: the headline
clock is anchored to a credential, and a documented credential is exactly what
is missing for self-taught and non-traditionally-educated founders.

The dropped rows are not concentrated in one region --- 13 of 28 are US-based,
against 48\% of the retained rows --- so the missingness does not appear to be a
proxy for country. But it is certainly not random with respect to education, and
its direction on the median is unknown rather than benign.

The venture clock is the one we would defend most strongly on coverage grounds:
\textbf{once someone starts the thing that eventually works, the median time to
\$10M-constant is 4.2 years}, and that figure rests on 98\% of eligible rows
rather than 77\%.

\subsection{The stopping rule fired on the boundary}

Collection stopped under a rule fixed before the last waves: median drift below
0.50 years across the two most recent waves, and CI half-width at most 1.0 year.
The final drift was (0.20, 0.00). The final half-width was \textbf{exactly
1.00}, against a test that fails only above 1.0.

Because that is uncomfortably close, we re-ran the bootstrap across 300 seeds.
The rule passes in \textbf{272 of 300 (90.7\%)}; the remaining 28 seeds give
[8.0, 11.0], a half-width of 1.50, and would not have stopped. The seed was
fixed in code in advance, so the stop is legitimate under the pre-specified
procedure, but it is a boundary stop and we report it as one. A reader who
prefers a stricter rule should treat collection as having ended arbitrarily at
$n=93$ and the interval as no tighter than [8.0, 11.0].

\section{Bias catalogue}

Twenty-four biases were catalogued during collection, several discovered only
after they had corrupted data. The ones that bear on the number:

\textbf{Survivorship, and it dominates everything else.} The sample is
conditioned on crossing. People who worked twenty years and never crossed are
absent by construction, not by rarity. This is not correctable within the design
and is the reason no odds statement can be made.

\textbf{Documentation bias, pushing short.} A crossing is datable when revenue
was published early. Companies that disclosed early skew toward faster growth
and better funding. Undatable crossings became exclusions rather than late
dates --- 30 of the 34 exclusions are undatable crossings --- so slow, quiet
successes drop out rather than lengthening the tail.

\textbf{Recall bias, corrected mid-study.} An early roster agent recalled names
and then found citations for them. Every row it produced had a valid citation
and the sample was still biased, because recall came first. Later waves
enumerate the list and read off it in published order.

\textbf{Anti-fame steering, the same error inverted.} Told to avoid names it
would have recalled, a later agent rejected Peter Thiel, Vinod Khosla and Jensen
Huang from enumerated lists as ``too obvious.'' That is the identical bias with
its sign flipped. The rule is that prominence is not a criterion in either
direction; the final wave took Phil Knight, Jensen Huang and John Hopfield in
rank order and logged the temptation to skip them.

\textbf{Correlated observations.} Co-founders of one company share one crossing,
and the bootstrap treats every row as independent, so a co-founder pair narrows
the interval without adding information. A rule of one row per hit \emph{event}
--- entity plus year --- was added to the frame and rejected five candidates on
its first use. One pair predating the rule remains, at Palm Computing in 1995;
both rows are on fallback basis and therefore sit outside the headline set
entirely, so they do not touch the reported interval.

\textbf{Wave 1 was collected under a different design.} Its composition was
deliberately steered before the money-only rule was adopted. The sample is a
mixture of two sampling designs and that discontinuity cannot be undone.

\section{Audit method}

Each wave was audited by re-researching a drawn sample of its rows
\textbf{blind}: a second pass that could not see the first pass's answers, run
in a scratch directory containing only the brief and the inflation tool, after
an early audit was found reading the first pass's cached files in a shared
directory.

Disagreement is scored as \emph{contradiction} --- two readings more than one
year apart that do not overlap. A wave voids above 0.10 and its names are retired
rather than reused.

Two rule changes were made during the study and both are disclosed here because
each loosened the test. First, bounded ranges that overlap count as agreement:
forcing a researcher to collapse an honest bracket to a single year to satisfy
the check was voiding good waves. Second, a miss --- one pass dating a row the
other could not --- stopped counting as disagreement. A six-platform blind
cross-check measured a single-pass miss rate near 25\%, so on ten audited rows
two or three misses arise from search thoroughness alone; counting them voided
waves that contained no wrong dates. Misses are now treated as rescue signals.

The final two audits returned contradiction rates of \textbf{0.000}.

\section{What we would not defend}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Annual recurring revenue may have been treated as calendar-year
revenue.} One row is dated from ARR; a blind second pass using GAAP revenue gave
a wider bracket that overlapped, so the audit did not flag it. Other SaaS rows
may be dated the same way and were never swept.
\item \textbf{Three rows rest on a single source} for the year that decides
them, with the alternative reading disclosed in the row's own notes.
\item \textbf{Seven open questions were never resolved.} One concerns a founding
year that, if corrected, would push a bounded range past the ten-year cap and
turn that row into an exclusion. That row is outside the headline set, so the
reported median does not move.
\item \textbf{Thirty-five of the earliest roster rows} predate the rule
requiring a membership URL naming the person.
\item \textbf{Collection was executed by language models} following written
briefs, with a human deciding methodology. The briefs, every batch log, every
audit pairing and every rejection are in the repository.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Among people who eventually built something that crossed \$10 million in
constant 2026 dollars, the median took \textbf{9.0 years from the end of formal
education}, 95\% CI [8.0, 10.0] --- but 13.0 years if the hit landed before
1995 and 8.0 if after, and \textbf{4.2 years from starting the venture that
worked}.

The most defensible reading is the narrowest one. This dataset measures how long
the apprenticeship ran for people whose apprenticeship ended in a hit. It cannot
say how many served one and got nothing, and the biases that survived
enumeration push the surviving figure short. Treat nine years as a floor on the
observed, not an expectation for the aspiring.
